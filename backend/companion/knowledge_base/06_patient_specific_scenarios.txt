COMMON DEMENTIA PATIENT SCENARIOS AND APPROPRIATE RESPONSES
Source: Alzheimer's Association, Dementia Care Central, clinical best practices

This document provides evidence-based response templates for the most common situations encountered with dementia patients. These are based on Validation Therapy, Reminiscence Therapy, and clinical communication guidelines.

SCENARIO: PATIENT ASKS "WHERE AM I?"
The patient is experiencing disorientation and may feel frightened.
Appropriate response approach:
1. Orient gently: "You're at home, in your living room. You're safe here."
2. Provide sensory anchoring: "Can you feel the soft chair? This is your favorite spot."
3. Offer comfort: "I'm right here with you."
Do NOT: quiz them on their location, express frustration, or provide excessive detail that overwhelms.

SCENARIO: PATIENT ASKS FOR A DECEASED LOVED ONE ("Where is my mother?")
The patient may be experiencing a memory from a different time period or may be feeling the need for comfort and safety that the person represented.
Appropriate response approach (middle/late stage):
1. Acknowledge the emotion: "You're thinking about your mother. She meant so much to you."
2. Use reminiscence: "Tell me about her. What was she like?"
3. Redirect if distress continues: "She always made you feel safe, didn't she? I'm here to take care of you right now."
Do NOT: say "She died" or "Don't you remember?" This can cause fresh grief every time that the person cannot process. Therapeutic fibbing (saying "She's resting" or "She'll be by later") is acceptable when the truth would cause repeated acute distress.
For early stage (if the person has awareness): a more honest approach may be appropriate, calibrated to their emotional state.

SCENARIO: PATIENT SAYS "I WANT TO GO HOME"
Even when the person is physically at home, "home" often represents safety, comfort, and a time in their life when they felt in control. It is not always a literal request.
Appropriate response approach:
1. Acknowledge the feeling: "You miss being at home. That's understandable."
2. Explore the emotion: "What do you miss most about home?"
3. Provide comfort: "I want you to feel safe and comfortable. Let me get your favorite blanket."
4. Redirect: "Tell me about the home you grew up in."
Do NOT: argue that they ARE home, show them proof, or dismiss the feeling.

SCENARIO: PATIENT REPEATS THE SAME QUESTION
This is caused by the short-term memory loop being broken. The person genuinely does not remember asking before.
Appropriate response approach:
1. Answer with the same words each time. Consistent phrasing provides comfort through familiarity.
2. Use a calm, patient tone identical to the first time they asked.
3. Address the underlying anxiety: "Dinner will be at 6 o'clock. You won't miss it — I'll come get you."
4. Redirect after answering: "Dinner is at 6. While we wait, would you like to look at these photos?"
Do NOT: say "You already asked that," show impatience, or change your answer.

SCENARIO: PATIENT DOES NOT RECOGNIZE FAMILY MEMBER
This is one of the most painful experiences for caregivers but is a normal progression of the disease.
Appropriate response approach:
1. Introduce yourself warmly each time: "Hi Dad, it's Sarah, your daughter. I've come to spend time with you."
2. Do not test or quiz: "Do you know who I am?" puts them in a position of failure.
3. Use familiar references: "I brought your favorite cookies, just like Mom used to make."
4. Focus on the emotional connection, not name recognition.
For the caregiver: validate their grief. "It's incredibly painful when he doesn't know you. That doesn't mean he doesn't feel your presence and love."

SCENARIO: PATIENT BECOMES AGITATED OR ANGRY
Agitation usually has a trigger. Common triggers include pain, overstimulation, feeling rushed, inability to communicate a need, environmental change, or medication effects.
Appropriate response approach:
1. Stay calm. Your calmness is contagious.
2. Lower your voice, slow your speech, keep your body language open and non-threatening.
3. Give them space. Don't touch them without permission when they are agitated.
4. Validate: "I can see you're upset. That's okay. I'm here to help."
5. Try to identify the cause: "Are you uncomfortable? Are you in pain?"
6. Redirect: offer a drink, suggest a walk, play music, change the environment.
Do NOT: argue, raise your voice, physically restrain, or take it personally.

SCENARIO: PATIENT REFUSES TO TAKE MEDICATION
Causes: forgetting what the medication is for, difficulty swallowing, bad taste, paranoia about being poisoned, too many pills.
Appropriate response approach:
1. Offer one medication at a time with a simple explanation: "This is your heart pill. The doctor wants you to take it."
2. Offer with food or drink to mask taste.
3. Ask the pharmacist about liquid, dissolvable, or patch alternatives.
4. If they refuse, do not force. Wait 15-20 minutes and try again with a different approach.
5. If consistent refusal, consult the doctor about which medications are essential and which can be discontinued.
Do NOT: crush medications into food without telling the person (unless specifically advised by the doctor) or argue about why they need to take it.

SCENARIO: PATIENT ACCUSES CAREGIVER OF STEALING
This is a common paranoid delusion, especially in moderate-stage Alzheimer's. The person misplaces items and, unable to remember where they put them, concludes someone stole them.
Appropriate response approach:
1. Don't take it personally. This is the disease, not a reflection of their trust in you.
2. Don't argue or try to prove you didn't take anything.
3. Help them search: "Let's look for your wallet together."
4. If you know where the item is, guide them to find it themselves: "Why don't you check the nightstand drawer?"
5. Keep duplicates of commonly lost items (glasses, keys) if possible.
6. Designate a consistent, visible spot for important items.
Do NOT: confront them with logic, get defensive, or display hurt feelings.

SCENARIO: PATIENT BELIEVES THEY NEED TO GO TO WORK
The person may be living in a memory of their working years. Work represented identity, purpose, and routine.
Appropriate response approach:
1. Acknowledge: "You've always been such a hard worker."
2. Redirect: "It's your day off today. You've earned a rest."
3. Offer purposeful activity: "Would you help me with this? I could really use your expertise." Give them a simple task that resembles their former work.
Do NOT: argue that they're retired or point out their age.

SCENARIO: PATIENT IS HAVING A LUCID MOMENT
Lucid moments are periods of unexpected clarity in moderate to late-stage dementia. They can be beautiful and also confusing for caregivers.
Appropriate response approach:
1. Enjoy the moment. Be present and responsive.
2. Do not overwhelm them with information or decisions.
3. Do not test their clarity by asking questions like "Do you know what day it is?"
4. Express love and connection: "It's so good to talk with you like this."
5. Be prepared for the lucidity to end. Do not show disappointment when it does.
For the caregiver: lucid moments near the end of life (terminal lucidity) can occur. While they provide comfort, they do not indicate recovery.

SCENARIO: PATIENT IS EXPERIENCING SUNDOWNING
The patient becomes increasingly confused, anxious, agitated, or combative as daylight fades in the late afternoon and evening.
Appropriate response approach:
1. Reduce stimulation. Turn off the television, reduce noise, dim bright overhead lights but keep the room well-lit with soft, warm lighting.
2. Offer a light snack with protein. Low blood sugar can worsen sundowning.
3. Speak in a slow, calm, reassuring voice.
4. Play soft, familiar music.
5. Offer a simple, repetitive activity: folding towels, sorting objects, looking at a photo album.
6. If the person wants to pace, let them walk in a safe area. Walking can reduce agitation.
7. Close curtains before it gets dark to prevent visual confusion from reflections.
Do NOT: argue, use overhead fluorescent lights, have the television on loudly, or try to reason with them about why they should calm down.
