DEMENTIA STAGES AND PROGRESSION
Source: Alzheimer's Association Clinical Guidelines, National Institute on Aging

MILD COGNITIVE IMPAIRMENT (MCI) - PRE-DEMENTIA
MCI is a stage between normal age-related cognitive changes and dementia. A person with MCI has noticeable memory or thinking problems that are greater than expected for their age but can still carry out everyday activities independently. Not all people with MCI develop dementia. Approximately 10-15% of people with MCI progress to dementia each year.
Key signs: forgetting recent conversations, misplacing items, difficulty finding words, trouble with planning and organization.
Care approach: encourage cognitive stimulation, maintain social engagement, establish routines, begin legal and financial planning while the person can still participate in decisions.

EARLY STAGE (MILD DEMENTIA)
Duration: typically 2-4 years after diagnosis.
The person can still function independently in many areas — they may drive, work, and participate in social activities. However, they begin to have noticeable difficulties.
Common symptoms: difficulty remembering recently learned information, forgetting names of new acquaintances, losing or misplacing objects, trouble with planning and organizing, getting lost in familiar places, difficulty managing finances, withdrawal from social situations due to awareness of changes.
Preserved abilities: long-term memory is largely intact, can hold conversations, recognizes family and friends, can perform self-care tasks, personality is mostly unchanged.
Emotional impact: the person is often aware of their declining abilities, leading to frustration, anxiety, depression, and embarrassment. This awareness is clinically significant — acknowledge their feelings without dismissing them.
Care approach: support independence where safe, use memory aids (calendars, notes, phone reminders), establish consistent daily routines, encourage physical exercise, maintain social connections, begin discussing future care preferences.

MIDDLE STAGE (MODERATE DEMENTIA)
Duration: this is typically the longest stage, lasting 2-10 years.
The person requires increasing assistance with daily activities. Behavioral and personality changes become more pronounced.
Common symptoms: increasing memory loss including personal history and significant life events, confusion about time, place, and events, difficulty recognizing family and friends, inability to learn new things, difficulty with language — trouble finding words, losing train of thought, needing help choosing appropriate clothing, increasing need for help with bathing, grooming, and toileting, wandering and getting lost, sleep disturbances, suspiciousness and paranoia, repetitive behaviors (asking the same question, packing and unpacking, hand-wringing), hallucinations (more common in Lewy body dementia), sundowning — increased confusion and agitation in late afternoon and evening.
Preserved abilities: can usually still eat independently, recognizes familiar faces even if names are forgotten, responds to music especially from their youth, retains procedural memories (how to walk, use a fork), emotional memory is preserved — they remember how people made them feel even when they forget the specifics.
Care approach: simplify communication (short sentences, yes/no questions), create a safe environment (remove tripping hazards, lock away dangerous items, install door alarms), maintain structured daily routine, use redirection for behavioral issues rather than confrontation, provide supervision for all activities, consider adult day programs for social stimulation.

LATE STAGE (SEVERE DEMENTIA)
Duration: typically 1-3 years.
The person becomes entirely dependent on caregivers for all basic activities of daily living. Communication is minimal.
Common symptoms: severe memory loss — may not recognize family members or themselves in a mirror, loss of awareness of recent experiences and surroundings, loss of ability to communicate verbally (may use single words or phrases, or become completely nonverbal), loss of ability to walk, sit, hold head up, or swallow, incontinence, increased vulnerability to infections especially pneumonia, weight loss, skin breakdown, seizures in some cases.
Preserved abilities: may respond to touch and familiar voices, can experience comfort from music, may show emotion through facial expressions or sounds, name recognition is one of the last abilities lost.
Care approach: focus on quality of life and dignity, provide gentle physical care (skin care, repositioning, mouth care), use soft touch and calm voice, play familiar music, ensure comfort and pain management, support family through grief and anticipatory loss, consider hospice and palliative care.

TYPES OF DEMENTIA AND THEIR DISTINCT FEATURES
Alzheimer's disease accounts for 60-80% of dementia cases. It is characterized by amyloid plaques and tau tangles in the brain. Memory loss is typically the earliest and most prominent symptom. Progression is gradual.
Vascular dementia is the second most common type, caused by reduced blood flow to the brain from stroke or small vessel disease. Symptoms depend on which brain areas are affected. Progression may be stepwise — sudden declines followed by periods of stability. Executive function (planning, organizing, decision-making) is often affected early.
Lewy body dementia is caused by abnormal protein deposits called Lewy bodies. Distinctive features include visual hallucinations (often detailed and recurring), fluctuating cognition (good days and bad days), REM sleep behavior disorder, Parkinsonian motor symptoms (tremor, rigidity, slow movement), sensitivity to antipsychotic medications — these drugs can be dangerous and even fatal for people with Lewy body dementia.
Frontotemporal dementia (FTD) affects personality, behavior, and language more than memory in early stages. Onset is often younger (40s-60s). Two main variants: behavioral variant (personality changes, disinhibition, apathy, compulsive behaviors) and language variant (progressive difficulty with speech, word-finding, or comprehension). Memory may be relatively preserved in early stages.
Mixed dementia involves two or more types occurring simultaneously, most commonly Alzheimer's and vascular dementia. This is more common than previously recognized. Symptoms may reflect a combination of the underlying diseases.
