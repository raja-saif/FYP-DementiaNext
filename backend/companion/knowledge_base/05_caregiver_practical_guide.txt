PRACTICAL CAREGIVER GUIDE: DAY-TO-DAY DEMENTIA CARE
Source: Alzheimer's Association, National Institute on Aging, Family Caregiver Alliance

DAILY ROUTINE AND STRUCTURE
A consistent daily routine is one of the most powerful tools for managing dementia. It reduces confusion, anxiety, and behavioral symptoms by creating predictability.
Guidelines for establishing routine:
1. Keep wake-up time, meal times, activity times, and bedtime consistent every day.
2. Schedule the most demanding tasks (bathing, doctor visits, outings) during the person's best time of day, which is usually morning.
3. Build in physical activity, social interaction, and rest periods.
4. Allow flexibility — the routine is a framework, not a rigid schedule. If the person is having a bad day, adapt.
5. Post the daily schedule in a visible location using simple words or pictures.
6. When the routine must change (doctor's appointment, visitor), prepare the person in advance with gentle reminders throughout the morning.

HOME SAFETY
Falls are the leading cause of injury in people with dementia. The home environment should be modified for safety.
General safety: remove throw rugs and clutter from walkways, install grab bars in bathroom near toilet and shower, ensure adequate lighting especially at night (motion-sensor lights in hallways and bathroom), remove or lock up guns, power tools, sharp knives, and toxic chemicals, install stove knob covers or auto-shutoff devices, lower water heater temperature to 120°F (49°C) to prevent scalding, remove locks on interior doors (the person could lock themselves in), install alarms on exterior doors.
Wandering prevention: install door alarms or motion detectors, use childproof covers on door handles, consider a GPS tracking device, hide car keys, keep a recent photograph available for search purposes, notify neighbors and local police about the person's condition, register with local wandering response services.
Bathroom safety: use non-skid bath mats inside and outside the tub, install a raised toilet seat, place colored tape on edges of steps or level changes, use a shower chair and handheld showerhead, keep the bathroom door unlocked from the outside, consider removing mirrors if the person does not recognize their reflection and becomes frightened.

NUTRITION AND HYDRATION
People with dementia are at high risk of malnutrition and dehydration. They may forget to eat or drink, lose the ability to recognize hunger or thirst, have difficulty preparing food, or develop swallowing problems.
Key guidelines:
1. Offer 6-8 small meals and snacks rather than 3 large meals.
2. Ensure adequate fluid intake — aim for 6-8 cups of liquid daily. Offer water, juice, and soup frequently. Dehydration can cause sudden confusion that mimics disease progression.
3. Use familiar, well-liked foods. This is not the time to introduce new diets.
4. Make eating easy: finger foods, adaptive utensils (weighted spoons, plate guards, non-slip mats), high-contrast colored plates.
5. Sit with the person during meals. Social eating encourages food intake.
6. If the person uses dentures, ensure they fit well and are clean.
7. Monitor weight weekly. Report significant weight loss (more than 5% in one month or 10% in six months) to the doctor.
8. In later stages, consult a speech-language pathologist for swallowing evaluation. Aspiration pneumonia from swallowing difficulties is a major cause of death in late-stage dementia.
9. High-calorie, nutrient-dense foods (avocados, nut butters, whole milk yogurt, cheese) help when appetite is poor.
10. Never force food. If the person consistently refuses to eat in late stages, this may be part of the natural dying process. Discuss with the healthcare team and consider palliative goals.

DRIVING AND DEMENTIA
Driving is one of the most contentious issues in dementia care because it involves both safety and independence.
When to stop driving: all people with moderate or severe dementia should not drive. For mild dementia, driving ability should be assessed on an individual basis. Warning signs include: getting lost on familiar routes, slow reaction times, confusing the brake and gas pedal, drifting between lanes, difficulty with turns or highway merging, near-misses or fender benders, forgetting the destination while driving.
How to approach the conversation: have the doctor recommend stopping — this shifts the blame away from the family. Offer alternative transportation solutions. If the person refuses to stop, consider disabling the car, hiding keys, or contacting the DMV for a mandatory driving evaluation.
Important: stopping driving is a major identity loss for many people. Acknowledge their grief and frustration.

LEGAL AND FINANCIAL PLANNING
These conversations should happen as early as possible while the person can still participate in decision-making.
Essential documents:
1. Durable Power of Attorney (financial) — designates someone to make financial decisions.
2. Healthcare Power of Attorney or Healthcare Proxy — designates someone to make medical decisions.
3. Living Will or Advance Directive — specifies wishes for end-of-life care (CPR, ventilator, feeding tube, hospitalization).
4. HIPAA Authorization — allows the designated person to access medical records and speak with healthcare providers.
5. Will — specifies how assets should be distributed.
6. Long-term care insurance review — understand what coverage exists for home care, adult day programs, assisted living, or memory care.
Recommendations: consult an elder law attorney who specializes in these issues. Complete all documents while the person can still demonstrate capacity. Store copies in a secure, accessible location. Inform all family members of the arrangements. Review and update documents periodically.

WORKING WITH HEALTHCARE PROVIDERS
Tips for effective doctor visits:
1. Prepare a written summary of symptoms, behaviors, medication changes, and concerns before the appointment.
2. Bring a complete list of all medications including over-the-counter and supplements.
3. Bring a second person to help listen and take notes.
4. Ask the doctor to explain the treatment plan in writing.
5. Report changes in behavior, sleep, eating, or function — these may indicate treatable conditions.
6. Ask specifically about medication interactions if multiple doctors are prescribing.
7. If you disagree with a recommendation, express your concerns. You are the expert on the person you care for.
When to seek urgent medical attention: sudden change in mental status (could indicate infection, stroke, or medication issue), falls with head injury, signs of dehydration (dark urine, dry mouth, confusion), fever, chest pain, difficulty breathing, sudden inability to walk or move one side of the body.

MANAGING INCONTINENCE
Incontinence (loss of bladder or bowel control) develops in middle to late stages for most people with dementia.
Causes: the person may not recognize the urge to go, may forget where the bathroom is, may not be able to undress in time, or may have a urinary tract infection.
Management:
1. Establish a toileting schedule — take the person to the bathroom every 2-3 hours.
2. Watch for signs they need to go: restlessness, pulling at clothing, facial expressions.
3. Make the bathroom easy to find: clear pathway, open door, light on, sign on the door.
4. Use clothing that is easy to remove: elastic waistbands, Velcro closures instead of buttons or zippers.
5. Use absorbent products discreetly — they should be introduced sensitively to preserve dignity.
6. At night, limit fluids after 6 PM and ensure a clear, lit path to the bathroom.
7. If incontinence develops suddenly, see the doctor — urinary tract infections are extremely common in dementia and can cause dramatic changes in cognition and behavior.
8. Maintain skin care to prevent irritation and breakdown.

PAIN MANAGEMENT IN DEMENTIA
People with dementia may not be able to articulate that they are in pain. Untreated pain is one of the most common causes of behavioral disturbance.
How to detect pain:
1. Watch for nonverbal cues: grimacing, guarding a body part, groaning, resisting movement, agitation, withdrawal, reduced appetite, disrupted sleep.
2. Use a pain assessment tool designed for nonverbal patients (PAINAD or Abbey Pain Scale).
3. Consider common pain sources: arthritis, dental problems, constipation, pressure sores, urinary tract infection, ill-fitting shoes or clothing.
Treatment:
1. Address the cause if possible.
2. Start with acetaminophen (Tylenol) as first-line pain medication — it is safe in appropriate doses.
3. Avoid NSAIDs (ibuprofen, naproxen) long-term due to gastrointestinal and cardiovascular risks, especially in elderly patients.
4. Opioids may be necessary for severe pain but carry risks of confusion, falls, and constipation.
5. Non-pharmacological approaches: warm or cold compresses, gentle massage, repositioning, distraction with music or activities.
6. Monitor and document pain regularly. Pain management should be an ongoing conversation with the healthcare team.

END-OF-LIFE CARE
Late-stage dementia is a terminal condition. The focus shifts from extending life to ensuring comfort and dignity.
Signs of approaching end of life: inability to eat or swallow, repeated infections (pneumonia), very limited or no verbal communication, inability to walk or sit unassisted, weight loss despite adequate food offerings.
Palliative and hospice care: hospice is available when the doctor certifies a life expectancy of 6 months or less. Hospice focuses on comfort rather than cure. It provides pain management, emotional and spiritual support for the patient and family, nursing care, medical equipment, and bereavement support for the family after death.
Decisions about feeding tubes: artificial nutrition (feeding tubes) does not improve survival or quality of life in late-stage dementia. It is associated with increased risk of aspiration, infection, and discomfort. Major medical organizations including the American Geriatrics Society recommend against feeding tubes for patients with advanced dementia. Careful hand-feeding of small amounts remains the preferred approach.
Family support: anticipatory grief is normal. Family members often feel guilt about decisions. Reassurance that they are providing good care and that their loved one is comfortable is critical. Bereavement support should continue after death.
