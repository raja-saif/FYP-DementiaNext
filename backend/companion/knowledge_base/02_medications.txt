DEMENTIA MEDICATIONS: CLINICAL REFERENCE
Source: NICE Guidelines TA217, APA Clinical Practice Guidelines, National Institute on Aging

CHOLINESTERASE INHIBITORS
These medications work by blocking the breakdown of acetylcholine, a neurotransmitter important for memory and learning. They do not cure dementia but can temporarily slow symptom progression in some patients.

DONEPEZIL (Brand name: Aricept)
Indication: mild, moderate, and severe Alzheimer's disease.
Dosage: typically starts at 5 mg once daily, may increase to 10 mg after 4-6 weeks. A 23 mg formulation exists for moderate to severe disease.
Administration: taken once daily at bedtime.
Common side effects: nausea, diarrhea, vomiting, loss of appetite, muscle cramps, fatigue, insomnia, vivid dreams.
Serious side effects: slow heart rate (bradycardia), fainting, gastrointestinal bleeding.
Drug interactions: NSAIDs (increased bleeding risk), anticholinergic medications (counteract the benefit), beta-blockers (increased risk of bradycardia).
Notes: most widely prescribed dementia medication. Generally well tolerated. Side effects often diminish after the first few weeks.

RIVASTIGMINE (Brand name: Exelon)
Indication: mild to moderate Alzheimer's disease and Parkinson's disease dementia.
Dosage: oral — starts at 1.5 mg twice daily, gradually increased to 6 mg twice daily. Transdermal patch — starts at 4.6 mg/24 hours, may increase to 9.5 mg/24 hours or 13.3 mg/24 hours.
Administration: taken with food (oral) or applied as a skin patch.
Common side effects: nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, weight loss, dizziness, headache. The transdermal patch causes fewer gastrointestinal side effects than oral form.
Serious side effects: severe vomiting, pancreatitis, allergic skin reactions with patch.
Notes: the only cholinesterase inhibitor available as a patch, which is beneficial for patients who have difficulty swallowing or have significant GI side effects. The only cholinesterase inhibitor approved for Parkinson's disease dementia.

GALANTAMINE (Brand name: Razadyne)
Indication: mild to moderate Alzheimer's disease.
Dosage: starts at 4 mg twice daily, may increase to 8 mg twice daily, then 12 mg twice daily. Extended-release capsule: 8 mg once daily, increasing to 16 mg then 24 mg once daily.
Administration: taken with meals.
Common side effects: nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, dizziness, headache, decreased appetite.
Serious side effects: slow heart rate, fainting, severe skin reactions.
Notes: has a dual mechanism — both inhibits acetylcholinesterase and modulates nicotinic receptors. Should not be used in severe hepatic or renal impairment.

MEMANTINE (Brand name: Namenda)
Indication: moderate to severe Alzheimer's disease.
Mechanism: NMDA receptor antagonist — works differently from cholinesterase inhibitors by regulating glutamate, another neurotransmitter involved in learning and memory. Excess glutamate can damage nerve cells.
Dosage: starts at 5 mg once daily, increased by 5 mg weekly to a target of 10 mg twice daily. Extended-release: starts at 7 mg once daily, target dose 28 mg once daily.
Administration: can be taken with or without food.
Common side effects: dizziness, headache, constipation, confusion, drowsiness.
Serious side effects: hallucinations, seizures (rare).
Drug interactions: other NMDA antagonists (amantadine, dextromethorphan), carbonic anhydrase inhibitors, sodium bicarbonate (alkaline urine increases memantine levels).
Notes: can be used in combination with cholinesterase inhibitors. The combination of Donepezil and Memantine is common for moderate to severe disease.

COMBINATION THERAPY
Donepezil + Memantine (Brand name: Namzaric) is available as a fixed-dose combination for moderate to severe Alzheimer's. Using both drug classes together may provide additional benefit compared to either alone.

IMPORTANT CAREGIVER GUIDANCE ON MEDICATIONS
Never stop dementia medications abruptly without consulting the doctor — sudden withdrawal can cause rapid cognitive decline that may not be recoverable.
Medications should be taken at the same time each day as part of a routine.
Monitor for side effects especially in the first 4-6 weeks after starting or increasing dose.
If the patient has difficulty swallowing pills, ask the doctor about liquid formulations, dissolvable tablets, or patches.
Dementia medications do not stop the disease from progressing — they temporarily manage symptoms. Families should be prepared for eventual decline despite medication.
Keep all medications locked away if the patient is confused about dosing — accidental double-dosing is a common safety concern.

MEDICATIONS TO AVOID OR USE WITH CAUTION IN DEMENTIA
Anticholinergic medications (diphenhydramine/Benadryl, oxybutynin, certain antidepressants) directly counteract cholinesterase inhibitors and can worsen confusion.
Benzodiazepines (diazepam, lorazepam) increase fall risk and can worsen confusion. They should be avoided if possible.
Antipsychotics carry a black box warning for increased mortality risk in elderly dementia patients. They should only be used when behavioral symptoms are severe and non-pharmacological approaches have failed. Antipsychotics are particularly dangerous in Lewy body dementia.
Over-the-counter sleep aids containing diphenhydramine should be avoided.
